0. The 30-Second Version#

  • The situation: the old trust system is failing as a whole; society has entered the credit-recasting period (the individual side calls it the “vacuum period” — the two sides of the same window).
  • The tool: a “signal → index → boundary” analytical framework. No predictions, no verdicts.
  • The stance: an individual doing verifiable things and occupying the “verifier” position is supplying raw material for the recasting.

⚠️ The one-sentence standing prohibition: do not read this as a doomsday prophecy — the recasting period is labor pain, not the end. Things are breaking, and things are growing.

I. The Individual Side Covered “How You Walk.” One Question Remains Unanswered#

Trust Crisis · Precondition Theory fully covered the individual half. Six gun muzzles, five appreciating assets, three rulers, plus one landing point: measure the world with Sartre’s ruler outward; guard yourself with Levinas’s line inward. It answered “how does a person walk in this era.”

But when readers close the book, another question most likely surfaces: changing currency is easy for me alone — but what about society? What state is the whole era actually in? Those collapses, blowups, plot reversals, self-proofs — taken together, are they a pile of coincidences, or a structure?

This essay answers that question. It is the mirror of the individual side: the individual side covered “where a person stands”; this covers “where the era is going.”

Rules first, so it isn’t misread. It is not current-affairs commentary — it takes no side and condemns no one. It is not a forecast — it cannot say whether tomorrow is better or worse. It is an observation framework: put the collective phenomena you see into it, and you know what they are, where they came from, where they tend, and what you can do. The framework is a tool, not a conclusion.

One term-alignment to pin down first: the individual side says “vacuum period”; the group side says “recasting period” — the two sides of the same window, not two events. Hold onto that window; the seven phenomena below are all the weather outside it.

II. The Phenomena First: What the Group Is Going Through#

Watch a month of trending topics on your phone and seven recurring shapes assemble themselves. They are not isolated news items; they are the seven symptoms of one illness.

1. Institutional credit went bankrupt first#

When an institution speaks, our first reaction is no longer “check whether it’s true” but “don’t swallow it whole.” The Evergrande real-estate collapse (2021 debt blowup) — the promises, contracts, and credit of a trillion-yuan enterprise went to zero overnight, and ordinary people’s life savings were zeroed out with them. Since then, the phrase “in black and white” carries less weight. This is the Tacitus Trap: once public credit is overdrawn far enough, nothing an institution says is believed. The organizational layer and the narrative layer are hemorrhaging as a whole.

2. Public opinion started flipping three times a day#

One event: one version in the morning, a reversal in the afternoon, another by night. The Yan Dongping case (a late-2024 Beijing divorce trial) — a one-sided narrative of “ten years of feeling” could turn ten years of sacrifice overnight into “a calculated scheme.” Truth is no longer discovered; it is “produced.” The group is pushed along by emotion; truth is diluted into “versions.” You cannot be sure which version is true — only that the next reversal is on its way.

3. The moral high ground collapsed systematically#

Even “doing good” is no longer credible by default. Han Hong’s charity has faced sustained questioning (since 2020 pandemic donations) — from where the money went to her motives, everything demands self-proof. Charities must prove innocence first; the cost of goodwill has been bid up to a historic high. Public welfare and charity, once emotional trusts, are now all on the “suspect first, verify later” list. When the moral high ground collapses, the people standing on it fall loudest.

4. People retreated into small screens#

Work units, neighborhoods, acquaintances — the old communities were our grandparents’ “trust containers”: your reputation and your credit had witnesses. Now these containers are dissolving; people have retreated into their separate little screens, and the entity they talk to most each day is the phone. Fewer people around who can vouch for you; more algorithm-fed echo chambers. Humans are physically closer and trust- lonelier.

5. Not hunger — “why should I”#

This generation’s material conditions are nowhere near the hunger of the previous one. Yet “lying flat” and “going-crazy literature” became a generation’s emotional outlets — not hunger, but suffocation. The gap between expectation and reality keeps widening: the rules say “everyone has a chance”; reality says “chance has a threshold.” Tocqueville pointed at this mechanism more than a century and a half ago: upheaval tends not to come at the poorest moment, but when life has just begun improving and expectations have just been raised — he was observing France at the end of the 18th century (the famous claim in The Old Regime and the Revolution), but the mechanism of “expectations lifted, reality lagging, tempers flaring” is recurring weather in this window. Expectations go up, reality can’t keep up, and the mood turns febrile.

6. No one dares move first#

Public affairs keep getting colder. Fundraisers must prove themselves repeatedly before anyone donates; no one takes the lead in community matters; everyone waits for someone else to move. This is not all apathy — in a trust-crisis era, the first mover bears all the risk while the benefits may be shared by all. So everyone tacitly chooses: wait. Collective action is paralyzed; free-riding becomes the default rationality. Olson called it “the collective action problem”: it is not that no one suffers — it is that no one has the incentive to pay the cost first.

7. Trust migrated from “institutions” to “persons”#

Trust in institutions collapsed, but trust must live somewhere. So it migrated onto “persons”: Luo Yonghao (founder of Smartisan, six hundred million in debt, repaid it live-stream by live-stream, the whole “true repayment” chronicle public) — paying your debts became this era’s scarcest credit asset. Various “conscientious entrepreneurs” and “grassroots idols” are celebrated not because their institutions are reliable but because they “seem like a person.” Meanwhile people split into camps by identity: fandoms, regions, professions, consumption patterns — when the whole society can’t be trusted, hug your own crowd first. Identity clustering is the psychological compensation of a trust crisis.

Seven symptoms, one mnemonic: institutions fall, opinion flips, morals crash, atoms scatter, the gap widens, no one moves, trust moves to persons. They are not seven accidental misfortunes — lay the structure out and you’ll find they point at one thing.

III. Not Chaos — the Old Trust System Is Failing#

Put the seven symptoms into a sociological index and their identities become clear:

SymptomCorresponding theoryRole it plays
Institutional credit bankruptcy / moral high-ground collapseDurkheim · anomieOld norms (integrity/goodwill/authority) collapsed, new ones not yet formed — structural necrosis of the trust infrastructure
Triple daily reversals / emotional contagionTarde · laws of imitation (+ Le Bon, with care)How ideas and emotions spread through crowds — transmission itself became the dominant force
Not hunger — “why should I”Tocqueville · expectation gapThe igniter — fairness breached, tempers febrile
No one dares move firstOlson · collective actionThe consequence — missing trust makes repair extremely hard
Trust migrating to “persons” / identity clusteringWeber · charisma / status groupsThe compensation — institutions failing, people retreat to persons and identity
(Background) rich-poor, class, distributionMarx · material institutionsThe base — always present, but rarely the direct detonator
(The plea) rebuild order, question legitimacyRousseau · social contractThe wish hanging overhead — and the easiest for power to exploit

The seven symptoms are not seven diseases; they are different faces of one lesion. Together, one sentence:

A trust crisis at the group level = anomie (Durkheim) × opinion contagion (Tarde) × expectation gap (Tocqueville), triple-stacked. The base is material institutions; the surface forms are paralyzed collective action and identity clustering; hanging overhead is the plea to “rebuild the contract.”

But note: this is a first-pass triage, not a diagnosis. One symptom can map to several indexes; one index can explain several symptoms; reality is a web — this one-to-one table is the reader’s starter version, not an exhaustive truth. For the general reader it also translates to one sentence:

Old norms rotted × rumors travel fast × everyone’s temper is fried = trust crisis. Material conditions are the base, paralyzed action the result, identity clustering the compensation.

This is the social picture behind “individuals changing currency”: the old trust-currency system is failing as a whole; society has entered the credit-recasting period. In the recasting period, public opinion is the fastest currency, and human sincerity is the most expensive raw material — and the greatest danger is someone trying to counterfeit currency in the recasting.

IV. Five Pits in This Framework, and One Most Dangerous Spot#

The framework is useful, but five places demand braking, or the tool becomes a weapon.

First: don’t use Le Bon to pass verdicts on groups. “The crowd,” “the mob,” “human nature is evil” — Le Bon’s theory is good at describing the group’s destructive face, but it is forbidden to slap it onto groups as a label and then rationalize “groups don’t deserve trust.” The destructive face is real; the constructive face is also real. Using the theory to pass verdicts is reading the medical chart and refusing to look at the patient.

Second: don’t drop the material base. Tarde’s account of idea contagion is excellent, but don’t let it eclipse material conditions. Rich-poor, class, distribution are forever the base — they rarely detonate directly, but they hold up everything above. Analysis that talks only about ideas and never about matter floats away.

Third: don’t use Tocqueville as a prediction. The expectation gap explains “under what conditions unrest tends to arise”; it does not prophesy “eruption is certain.” He observed France at the end of the 18th century — an era without today’s media and institutions. The mechanism can be borrowed; the conclusions cannot be transported. Conditions in place do not equal event occurring — countless variables sit in between. Using it as a doomsday countdown is misuse.

Fourth: watch for the “general will” being hijacked. This is the most dangerous one. Rousseau said the social contract flows from the general will — but the general will is easily stolen by power. In a trust crisis, the people shouting “rebuild the social contract, restore public credibility” do not all want rebuilding — some want to use the crisis to seize power. Any narrative that channels “distrust” toward “we need a strongman to take charge” deserves one extra question: who benefits? And one further: all narratives that fly the flag of “rebuilding trust” while demanding the transfer of power get one question first — who supervises the “rebuilder”?

Fifth, the general rule: don’t read this as a doomsday prophecy. The credit-recasting period is the labor pain of one paradigm replacing another, not the end of civilization. Things are breaking: the old trust infrastructure is collapsing. Things are growing: verifiability, sincerity, eye-level communication, small trust circles — the new trust assets are appreciating (the five currents on the individual side are exactly these). The framework is an observation tool, not a disaster prophecy; whoever uses it to argue “society is finished” has used it wrong.

And one last line drawing the competence boundary: this framework applies only to group phenomena at the level of social trust. It does not apply to legal determinations or individual moral verdicts. It is an observation lens, not a judge.

V. The Group Recasts — What Does the Individual Do?#

Society covered, we must return to you. Readers of this site are not here to watch the spectacle; they are here for “what should I do” — however clearly the group half is explained, if it doesn’t land on the individual it is just another way of reading the news.

The recasting will not complete itself. Society does not become trustworthy on its own unless a new consensus base appears — and that base has a name: verifiability.

But a key clarification first, so the logic doesn’t collide: an individual doing trustworthy things does not automatically aggregate into social consensus — otherwise the “collective action problem” wouldn’t exist (that is exactly what Olson said in chapter two: individually rational actions do not automatically sum into collective outcomes).

Aggregation happens at a smaller scale: small trust circles. Inside a small circle, your trustworthy behavior is directly seen and directly rewarded — the free-rider problem doesn’t exist inside the circle; when you’re honest, circle members know at once and reciprocate at once. Between circles, connection and amplification happen through “verifiers”: a person verified by one circle is handed to another circle for further verification. So read the “rebar” metaphor this way: rebar doesn’t become a building by lying scattered on the ground — it first becomes rebar inside small structures, then gets welded together node by node by verifiers.

This is precisely the group projection of the individual side’s five currents: current four (small trust circles appreciate) handles “becoming rebar”; current five (occupying the verifier position) handles “welding.” Every new trust niche you occupy at the individual level — one transparent deal, one promise given without discount, one thing made public before it charges — is one piece of rebar in the recasting period.

One sentence to close:

Individuals are changing currency; the group awaits recasting. Every old banknote you exchange adds one load of material to society’s recasting; every new trust niche you raise is one piece of rebar for the recasting period — first becoming rebar in small circles, then welded together by verifiers.

VI. Era Observation Starts From This Essay#

This essay does not end on a conclusion. It ends at the beginning of a column.

What “Era Observation” will do is apply this framework over and over: no chasing hot-topic emotions; only analytical frameworks. Every collective event that leaves you “unable to read it” or “suffocating” can be placed into this signal → index → calibration → boundary structure, and taken apart for you — what it is, where it came from, where it goes, what you can do. It makes no prophecies. It gives you a ruler, and a lamp.

The column’s rhythm, with the rule stated plainly — target: one issue per month, with a floor: better late than padded. A column about trustworthiness owes its first act of trustworthiness to itself: never submit a half-finished draft.
